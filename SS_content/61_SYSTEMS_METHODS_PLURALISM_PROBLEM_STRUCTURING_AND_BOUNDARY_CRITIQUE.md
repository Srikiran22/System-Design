# 61 — Systems methods, methodological pluralism and boundary critique

## Why this volume exists

Systems thinking is not a single method. It includes distinct traditions with different assumptions about what a system is, what counts as evidence, how stakeholders participate, and what “improvement” means. Selecting a method is itself a design decision. A quantitative simulation cannot settle a disagreement about whose goals count; a participatory workshop cannot by itself establish numerical reliability.

## The five approaches in the Open University synthesis

The Open University chapter groups five approaches used to work with complex situations of change: **System Dynamics (SD), Viable System Model (VSM), Strategic Options Development and Analysis (SODA), Soft Systems Methodology (SSM), and Critical Systems Heuristics (CSH)** [S180]. They are complementary, not interchangeable.

## SSM: disciplined learning, not a linear process flow

A commonly taught seven-stage representation is: explore the problematic situation; express it (often with a rich picture); formulate relevant system/root definitions; build conceptual models of purposeful activity; compare models with perceived reality; identify feasible and desirable changes; and take action. The seven-stage diagram is a learning device, not a mandatory waterfall sequence. SSM distinguishes a conceptual model from an empirical description of the organization. Root definitions can use **CATWOE**: Customers, Actors, Transformation, Weltanschauung (worldview), Owners, and Environmental constraints. The Checkland retrospective and an introductory review provide the method's historical and conceptual background [S181, S182].

## SODA and cognitive mapping

SODA uses participants' natural language to build means–ends maps. A construct is often expressed as a bipolar distinction (for example, “centralized approval” versus “local autonomy”). A facilitator elicits individual maps, explores causal/strategic links, and supports group negotiation toward options and action. The output is not a forecast; it is a transparent model of how the participants understand the situation and the consequences they anticipate [S183].

## VSM: organizational viability

Stafford Beer's Viable System Model is commonly represented as five interacting functions: **System 1** operational units; **System 2** coordination; **System 3** operational control and resource allocation; **System 4** intelligence/adaptation; and **System 5** identity, policy and ultimate balance. The model is recursive: a viable unit may itself contain viable systems. Use it to ask whether autonomy, coordination, control, intelligence and identity are adequately represented at each level. Avoid equating System 3 with ordinary middle management or assuming the labels map one-to-one to job titles [S184].

## CSH and boundary critique

Boundary critique makes explicit the value judgments that decide what is “in the system” and what is treated as an external condition. A widely used CSH heuristic asks questions across four sources of influence:

1. **Motivation:** who is the intended beneficiary; what is the purpose; what counts as success?
2. **Control:** who has decision power; what resources and conditions are controlled; who owns the system?
3. **Knowledge:** whose expertise and experience count; what knowledge is excluded; who is treated as an expert?
4. **Legitimacy:** who can challenge decisions; who bears consequences without representation; what mechanisms provide voice?

For each source, compare the actual situation with an explicit “ought” or idealized design. The purpose is not to discover a neutral boundary. It is to expose the consequences of boundary choices and make their ethical/political assumptions contestable [S185].

## Method selection and combination

Use SD when feedback and time behaviour dominate; SSM when the problem itself is disputed; SODA when negotiation across multiple problem owners is central; VSM when organizational viability and coordination are the focus; CSH when boundary, power and legitimacy questions matter. A combined intervention might use SSM to structure the situation, SODA to elicit and negotiate options, CSH to interrogate whose interests the options privilege, SD to test dynamic consequences, and VSM to examine organizational capability to sustain change. The sequence should be adapted to the context, not treated as a fixed recipe.

## Quality controls

- Record who participated, who did not, and how participants were selected.
- Preserve original stakeholder language alongside analyst interpretations.
- Mark assumptions and contested causal links; do not turn them into facts by drawing arrows.
- Distinguish descriptive maps, normative models and predictive models.
- Test quantitative models against historical behaviour, expert challenge and sensitivity analysis.
- Record what changed after the intervention and who experienced the consequences.
